The challenge

Falls are the leading cause of injury and injury death for older Australians. We know how to prevent many of them. In most regional communities, the prevention never arrives.

The scale of falls

In New South Wales, about 41,000 people are admitted to hospital after a fall each year. More than 1,200 die. Across Australia, falls cost the health system somewhere between A$4.3 and A$5 billion a year.

Those are the countable costs. The uncountable ones matter more to the person who falls. A fractured hip at 82 often means a hospital stay a long way from home, then rehabilitation, then a decision about whether returning home is realistic. Many people never get their confidence back. Some stop going out.

It gets larger from here

The population is ageing, and the numbers follow. On demographic change alone, NSW fall hospitalisations are projected to be about three times higher by 2051. If the trends of recent years continue rather than flattening, the projection is more than ten times higher. Transfers into aged care following a fall are projected to rise 3.2 times over the same period.

No plausible increase in hospital capacity absorbs that. The alternative is to reduce how many falls happen in the first place.

Regional patients do slightly worse, and travel much further

Once admitted, rural and urban fall patients in NSW have broadly similar outcomes: 5.0% of rural patients die within 30 days, against 4.9% in cities, and 18.9% are readmitted within 28 days, against 17.0%. The gap is small. What is not small is the distance travelled, the time away from home, and how much harder it is to get back to normal life afterwards.

Why prevention doesn't reach regional communities

Falls prevention is not an unsolved problem. Structured balance and strength programs reduce falls, and have done so consistently across decades of trials. NSW has run one of them, Stepping On, at real scale.

The constraint is not knowledge. It is delivery.

The workforce constraint

Most evidence-based falls programs are designed to be delivered by a health professional — usually a physiotherapist or exercise physiologist — running a class each week for a fixed block of sessions. That design works where those professionals are plentiful. Outside the major centres, they are not.

A regional physiotherapist's week is already committed. Adding a weekly community class means removing something else. So the class runs where there is spare clinical capacity, which is largely where the population is dense. Communities with a single practice, or none, get nothing.

There is a second problem, and it is about duration. Most professionally delivered programs run in blocks — seven weeks, ten weeks — because that is what can be funded and staffed. Balance is not like that. Strength and balance decline again once training stops. A program that ends after ten weeks produces a temporary benefit and then hands the person back to the same risk.

This is a design problem, not a performance problem

Nothing on this page is a criticism of rural clinicians. Rural and regional clinicians carry heavier caseloads, with less backup, less specialist support and longer distances than their metropolitan colleagues. They are not failing to run these programs through any lack of skill or will. They are fully occupied.

If a model requires scarce clinical hours to deliver every class, every week, indefinitely, then that model will not reach most regional communities — no matter how good the clinicians are or how hard they work. The model is what needs to change.

What would have to be true instead

A program that reaches regional NSW and stays there has to satisfy four conditions at once:

  • It runs without a clinician in the room every week, because those hours do not exist.
  • It is ongoing, not a block, because the benefit stops when the exercise stops.
  • It is clinically governed, because safety and progression are genuine clinical questions.
  • It is cheap enough to run in a town of 900 people.

That combination is what the model on the next page is built to deliver.
