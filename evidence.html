Does it work?

Structured balance and strength programs reduce falls. That is well established. The question this page answers is narrower: what happens when volunteers rather than paid professionals deliver them. Four programs give us most of what we know, and the limitations are set out at the bottom of the page.

Steady As You Go — New Zealand

Steady As You Go, run by Age Concern Otago, is the closest existing model to what is proposed here. Classes are peer-led: an older person from the group leads the weekly session, with physiotherapist oversight of the program. Classes run continuously rather than in blocks.

Its most useful result is not a falls figure. It is a duration figure. Across the Age Concern Otago cohort, participants stayed an average of 4.3 years, with a range from one to ten years.

That number matters more than it first appears. Balance training only protects you while you keep doing it. A ten-week block delivered by a physiotherapist may produce a larger short-term gain than a volunteer-led class, and still leave the person worse off three years later, because the block ended and the class did not.

A Matter of Balance — United States

A Matter of Balance began as a clinician-delivered program and was subsequently redesigned for lay leaders, as A Matter of Balance: Volunteer Lay Leader Model. It addresses fear of falling and the activity restriction that follows it — the habit of giving up walking, gardening or going out because of a worry about falling, which then weakens the person further.

Its relevance here is the translation itself. It is a worked example of an established program being deliberately re-engineered so that trained community volunteers could deliver it, and then disseminated widely through community agencies on that basis. The redesign was the mechanism that let it spread.

FallFitness — Sweden

FallFitness is a Swedish community fall-prevention exercise program studied in the form most relevant to us: comparing delivery by trained laypeople with delivery by health professionals.

It is the clearest existing precedent for the central design choice in this proposal — that the person leading the room each week does not have to be a clinician, provided the program they are leading was built by one and they were trained to lead it.

Stepping On — New South Wales

Stepping On is the NSW program, and the local benchmark. In the original randomised trial it reduced falls by 31%.

It was then scaled across the state. Between 2009 and 2014 it ran at 1,077 sites and reached 10,096 participants. That is a genuine achievement and shows there is community appetite for this kind of program in NSW.

Stepping On is a seven-week block, facilitated by a trained leader with clinical input. The proposal here is not a replacement for it. It is what happens in the weeks after week seven, and in the towns the blocks do not reach.

What it costs, compared with what it saves

Limitations, stated plainly

A page that only sells is not evidence. Here is what this evidence does not show.

No trial has directly compared volunteer-led with clinician-led delivery on falls outcomes

This is the central gap. We have good trials of clinician-delivered programs. We have good operational experience of volunteer-delivered programs. We do not have a head-to-head randomised comparison measuring falls in both arms. Anyone who tells you the volunteer model is proven equivalent is going beyond the evidence.

It is plausible that clinician-led delivery produces better short-term results

A physiotherapist can individualise more precisely, progress more aggressively and spot problems a trained volunteer would miss. If the comparison were ten weeks of each, we would expect the clinician-led class to come out ahead.

The programs described here differ from each other, and from this proposal

They differ in content, in intensity, in how much clinical oversight they carry and in the populations they served. Evidence from one is suggestive for another, not transferable to it.

The economic figures are indicative

They come from different countries, different health systems and different years, converted at a single nominal exchange rate. They support the claim that volunteer-led delivery is substantially cheaper per unit of benefit. They do not support a precise Australian cost estimate.

Why we think the case still holds

The relevant comparison in most regional communities is not volunteer-led against clinician-led. Clinician-led weekly classes are not available in those communities and are not going to become available, for the reasons set out in the challenge.

The real comparison is a volunteer-led class that runs every week for years, against no program at all. On that comparison the evidence is much less ambiguous. And a program people stay in for an average of 4.3 years has a long time to work in.

The full reasoning, with sources, is in the evidence paper.
