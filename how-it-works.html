How it works

Two tiers. Volunteers from the community deliver the class each week. Clinicians design what happens in it, train the leaders, review progression and take referrals when something changes. Neither tier works without the other.

The two-tier structure

What a class actually looks like

Where
A local hall, church meeting room, Men's Shed, neighbourhood centre or community club. Somewhere people already go. No gym, no equipment beyond sturdy chairs.
When
Once a week, at the same time each week, for one hour.
For how long
Indefinitely. This is the point. There is no graduation and no final session. In the New Zealand program this model is based on, people stay an average of 4.3 years, and some stay ten.
Who leads it
One or two trained volunteers from the community. Usually older people themselves, often participants who have been coming for a while.
What happens
A warm-up, then a set sequence of balance and leg-strength exercises done standing, with a chair to hold. Exercises are progressed in small steps as people get steadier. Most of it can be done seated if that is what someone needs.
What else happens
Tea afterwards. This is not decoration. Turning up every week for years is a social act before it is a clinical one, and the programs that last are the ones people enjoy.
What it costs the participant
A small contribution towards hall hire and tea, set by the host organisation. Nobody is turned away over cost.

The four points of clinical input

Clinical time is the scarcest resource in a regional community, so the model spends it only where it changes safety or effectiveness. There are four such points.

  1. Content design

    A physiotherapist sets the exercise sequence, the progression steps and the limits — what is in the program, what is not, and how a leader moves somebody up a level. This is done once, then reviewed. It is not repeated for each site.

  2. Leader training and accreditation

    Volunteer leaders are trained by a clinician before they lead a class, and accredited. Training covers the exercises, how to adapt them, how to spot someone who should not be exercising that day, and exactly when to stop and refer.

  3. Periodic progression review

    A clinician visits the class on a set schedule — typically quarterly once a site is established — to watch the group, check that progression is appropriate and answer the leaders' questions.

  4. Escalation and referral

    Leaders have a named clinician to contact and a written threshold for when to do so: a change in someone's balance, a fall at home, new dizziness, a medication change, anything that does not look right. The leader's job is to notice and refer, never to diagnose or treat.

What a volunteer leader does not do

Leaders do not assess, diagnose, treat, give advice about medication, or design exercises of their own. They run a set program, adapt it within limits they have been trained on, and refer when something changes. The boundary is written down, covered in training, and reinforced at every review visit.

Is it safe? Who is responsible?

These are the right questions to ask, and they deserve direct answers.

Screening before joining

Everyone completes a short health screen before their first class. Anyone with conditions outside the program's scope — unstable cardiac disease, acute injury, certain neurological conditions — is referred to their GP or physiotherapist before joining, not simply turned away. The screening form and the exclusion criteria are set by the clinical governance tier, not by the volunteers.

Where responsibility sits

The host organisation is responsible for the venue, the participant records, the incident reporting process and the insurance. The clinical governance tier is responsible for what is in the program and for whether a leader is accredited to run it. The volunteer leader is responsible for running the program as trained and for escalating when they should. Each of these is written into the site agreement so nobody is guessing.

Insurance and indemnity

The host organisation's public liability and volunteer insurance covers the class, as it covers its other activities, provided the class is a recognised activity of the organisation. Volunteers are covered as volunteers of the host, not as independent practitioners. Clinicians providing governance are acting within their normal scope, and this is the arrangement their existing professional indemnity is written to cover — but each clinician should confirm the position with their own insurer before signing on, and the program will supply a written role description for that purpose.

Incidents

Every fall, near miss or unexpected symptom during a class is recorded on a one-page form and sent to the clinical governance tier. The forms are reviewed together, not just individually, so that a pattern at one site or across sites is visible early.

The honest comparison

Exercise carries some risk, and a program that claims otherwise should not be trusted. The comparison that matters is not between this class and a perfectly safe alternative. It is between this class and what most regional communities have now, which is no falls prevention program at all, and a population getting less steady each year.
